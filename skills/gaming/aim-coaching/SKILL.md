# Aim Coaching Skill

Turn coach notes and player feedback into cautious, evidence-based motor diagnoses and sustainable aim practice. Match drills to the actual movement geometry, verify scenarios in KovaaK's official database, and account for the player's game workload instead of stacking aim volume automatically.

## When to Use
- Use for aim-coaching notes, VOD observations, player-reported aim errors, and KovaaK's/Overwatch practice plans.
- Don't treat subjective labels such as “bad reading” or “shaky” as a diagnosis without observable evidence.

## Player setup (standing facts)
- **Sensitivity:** 20cm/360. High sens — fingertips are forced into micro corrections; pad space is not the constraint. Tension spikes produce ~1.8x the crosshair displacement of a lower-sens player (34cm), so perception accuracy and tension control are amplified stakes.
- **Mouse pad:** Wallhack SP-004.
- **Primary game:** Overwatch 2 (Tracer/Soldier 76 tracking). Also plays FFA on Aim Arena (OW physics).
- **Confirmed diagnosis (Oct 11):** Grip spikes are perception-driven, not mechanics-driven. VAXTA (clean perception) = no spike. FFA Aim Arena (unpredictability + pressure + scanning load) = spike. Same game, same engine, same sensitivity — the difference is perceptual load from player unpredictability, competitive pressure, and active engagement, not ability visual clutter.

## Procedure
1. **Extract observations.** Separate what the coach/player directly observed (overshoot, shake, delayed correction, finger clenching, tension growing over a match) from explanations (“reading issue,” joint-use theory, or presumed root cause). State hypotheses as hypotheses; self-report alone cannot establish causation.
2. **Classify the motor skill (Gentile, 2000).** Is the in-game task discrete (static clicking, dynamic click-timing) or continuous (tracking, visual pursuit)? Mercy tracking is continuous. Discrete and continuous skills use different motor programs (PMC3773509) — do not train one to fix the other. Describe a technique as plain task phases, not a coined label or a named player's style. For static clicking, use neutral language such as “initial movement → small correction to finish alignment → confirm alignment → click.” Treat the correction as the usual step; omit it only when the initial movement lands accurately enough to confirm. Do not imply the initial movement alone should routinely land perfectly, or tell the player to flick/snap. For dynamic click-timing, preserve the specific rule: smooth pre-tracking and a silent click, never flicking or snapping. When building speed, improve the whole sequence by removing unnecessary path, corrections, or pauses while preserving alignment and accuracy—not by rushing one phase in isolation.
3. **Identify the target movement pattern.** What shape does the target trace through space? (Leap, bounce, strafe, air/3D, smooth arc, centering/orbit.) This is the primary scenario-selection filter because specificity of practice (Schmidt, 1975) drives transfer — the practiced movement pattern must match the target task's kinematic demands. See the movement-pattern keyword table in `references/weekly-routine.md`.
4. **Identify the smallest testable bottleneck.** Look for when the error begins: target acceleration, reversal, wide displacement, vertical motion, WASD input, session duration, or accumulated fatigue. For reactive tracking, distinguish lag, overshoot at reversals, reacquisition loss, axis-specific errors, and player-input coupling; do not collapse these into generic “slow reaction time.” Do not claim a visual deficit is disproven by errors in other scenarios; scenario performance alone rarely isolates perception from mechanics. Apply the neutral, evidence-calibrated comparison in `references/reactive-tracking-evidence.md`; don't center named players or playstyles.
5. **Ask the player about prior practice before evaluating a routine.** For playlist reviews, routine checks, or coach-note analysis, do not jump straight from the coach's observation to a diagnosis. Use any already-shared experience first, then ask a small number of targeted questions about what the player felt during/after practice, exactly when tension or loss of control began, what target/player movement was happening, and what changed in-game. Avoid asking for information already supplied; distinguish their felt experience from their interpretation of the cause. A player description is valid evidence; gameplay VOD is optional, not a prerequisite. If the user explicitly wants an immediate provisional read, give it with uncertainty and ask follow-up questions for refinement.
5a. **Use experiential diagnosis, not observational prescription.** When a coach VOD is available (MattyOW, Voltaic, etc.), extract the coach's method: did they play the scenario at the player's sensitivity to feel the bottleneck experientially, or did they observe from outside and prescribe by category? MattyOW's method is experiential — he plays the scenario, feels the space exhaustion or perception overload at the player's sens, then prescribes an easier variation that isolates the specific broken fundamental. PSEV's method is observational — diagnose from gameplay, prescribe by category. When the player reports what they felt during a scenario, that report IS the experiential diagnostic — they are inside the scenario the way MattyOW is. Treat their felt report as primary evidence for which fundamental is breaking, not as secondary to category theory. When a player identifies a specific felt experience ("VAXTA feels easier than in-game"), trace the root cause from that experience rather than fitting it to a pre-existing category.
5b. **When transcribing coach VODs, cross-check scenario names against the actual playlist.** Whisper transcription (especially the tiny model on CPU) garbles scenario names — "Aethercontrol Easy" becomes "Aether Control Easy," "BounceSphere Goated Easy" becomes "Bounce Sphere Shotgun," and general speech about tracking becomes fake scenario names like "Swab Track" or "Lay a Sphere." Always ask the player for the playlist screenshot or the exact scenario names after transcription; do not cache garbled names as real scenarios in `references/scenario-cache.md`. Mark transcription-derived scenario names as unverified until confirmed against the actual playlist or KovaaK's official database. Download Twitch VODs with `yt-dlp -f Audio_Only` and transcribe with `faster-whisper` (tiny model, CPU, int8) — the medium model requires CUDA/cuBLAS which may not be available. Run long transcriptions in the background (`terminal background=true notify=true`) and clean up temp audio files after extraction.
6. **Select one physical skill class per session, and span the speed-matching range within it.** Map target geometry and motor demand to the Viscose taxonomy in `references/weekly-routine.md`; never substitute a different class merely to fill a drill slot. Reserve blending for cleared isolated gates. Use Viscose classes as scenario-selection shorthand (which joint complex carries the movement), but ground the diagnostic reasoning in motor learning science (specificity, variable practice, practice load) — not in aim-community buzzwords as the causal model. **Speed-matching is the core skill and it is multi-dimensional** (slow/fast/ground/air). Do not collapse it into "smooth = slow only." Foundation skills should span the range: a slow constant-speed baseline → a variable-speed middle layer → a variable-speed + precision ceiling. This ensures the base carries into reactive: when direction changes are added later, the player can "pick up the pace" because fast speed-matching is already calibrated. The VSS (Variable Speed Strafes) family is the ideal variable-speed foundation scenario: the bot changes strafe SPEED (not direction abruptly), giving processing time to feel and calibrate tension to match velocity. Smoothness is the condition that gives the player time to calibrate, not the skill itself — the skill is reading velocity and matching tension across a range of speeds. **Perception framing (Oct 11, from MattyOW VOD):** grip spikes are downstream of perceptual overload — in-game visual complexity makes target speed feel faster than it is → tension overshoots to match perceived (not actual) speed. At high sensitivity (20cm/360), tension spikes produce ~1.8x crosshair displacement vs lower-sens players, so perception accuracy is amplified stakes. The Skill 1 → in-game progression is understood as progressively adding perceptual load, not just reactivity or speed — the foundation builds the perceptual baseline that makes in-game visual complexity survivable. Cue: "The movement is slower than it feels — read the actual speed, match tension to that. If your grip tightens, the target didn't get faster, your perception did."
7. **Scale and verify scenarios.** Use the new `Recommended KovaaKs Scenarios` sheet's archetype-specific difficulty tiers, chosen from the observed bottleneck and the current variation's challenge/purpose; no player Voltaic rank is required. Treat Novice/Intermediate/Advanced/Elite as scenario-scaling bands, not user labels. Do not use the prior Voltaic scenario spreadsheet. Search candidates through the official general interface at `https://kovaaks.com/kovaaks/scenarios`, using compact family keywords and movement-pattern keywords (leap, bounce, strafe, air, smooth, centering); broaden any query returning fewer than 10 results. Confirm exact active title, task geometry, player movement, and variant settings. Progress within the same archetype when an easier variation no longer challenges the player or has served its purpose and control remains stable; increase one demand at a time. Report official queries and links.
8. **Build the dose around the whole week.** Ask/consider whether the player also does VAXTA, FFA, or QP. Keep the requested 15-minute focused block (three same-class variations, five minutes each) only when it fits their workload. Three same-class variants are a practical way to add controlled variability, not a guaranteed contextual-interference benefit. Evidence on transfer varies by task and setting; keep variants within the required movement pattern and skill demand, and use them only while control remains stable (see `references/reactive-tracking-evidence.md`). Treat an existing 20-minute VAXTA practice as the OW2 translation bridge; do not automatically append another five-minute bridge. Prefer KovaaK's on days without FFA/QP when the player reports tension accumulation.
9. **Ramp one variable at a time.** Start with the easiest verified scenario/speed the player can control. Keep the cue external and simple (smoothly match target motion); avoid simultaneous joint-routing instructions. Apply the grip ceiling/reset/speed gates in the reference when appropriate. Do not invent precision thresholds such as overshoot radii or claim exact “percent speed” settings exist unless verified in the scenario.
10. **Close with a short, actionable plan.** Match answer length to the request: brief symptom update gets a brief adjustment; a requested weekly program gets the full day-by-day structure, progression gates, and OW2 bridge.

## Pitfalls
- **Do not turn a plausible mechanism into a certain diagnosis.** Tension can accompany degraded tracking without proving it caused every miss or excluding a reading component.
- **Do not prescribe more aim work after fatigue signs without checking recovery.** Finger clenching, rising tension, and deteriorating tracking are cues to pause or end that block, not to grind through it.
- **Do not stack 15 minutes of KovaaK's, 20 minutes of VAXTA, and FFA/QP by default.** Total workload and the player's reported deterioration matter more than completing every protocol component.
- **Do not require shoulder/wrist/finger micromanagement or continuous finger motion.** Use one low-tension cue and let the player report whether movement stays relaxed.
- **Do not confuse a lack of variable strafe speeds with a bot-death problem.** A scenario with the right dominant geometry (e.g., long strafes at ~80% accuracy) can still fail to train speed-matching if every bot strafes at the same speed — the tension profile is monotonous, there is nothing to transition, and the player gets no reps reading and matching different velocities. The invincible variant of the same scenario is equally useless: same speed, no death, no variation. The real fix is a scenario with variable strafe speeds (e.g., VSS family) that forces the player to read each bot's velocity and calibrate tension dynamically. Bot death/respawn is a separate transition demand that belongs in the reactive skill phase, not the speed-matching foundation.
- **Do not treat grip spikes as a pure mechanical deficit.** Grip spikes in-game are downstream of three FFA-specific loads that KovaaK's does not replicate: (1) **unpredictability** — players juke, dash, stop, and reverse without telegraph, forcing reactive tension calibration instead of pre-read tension matching; (2) **competitive pressure** — getting shot at raises physiological arousal and baseline tension, leaving less headroom before a spike; (3) **active scanning + engagement load** — the player must scan for targets, decide to engage, and track through character movement-ability geometry changes (dash, blink, wall-ride) that bots don't have. VAXTA has none of these; FFA Aim Arena has all three at the same sensitivity and engine — the confirmation that the spike is perception-driven, not mechanics-driven. At high sensitivity (20cm/360), each mm of jitter produces ~1.8x the crosshair displacement of a lower-sens player (34cm), so the same tension spike is amplified. The fix is not "relax your grip" — it is recognizing that the movement is slower than it feels and is fingertip-controllable. The Skill 1 → Skill 2 → in-game progression is understood as progressively adding these loads: Skill 1 trains pre-read speed calibration in a zero-pressure, predictable, single-target environment; Skill 2 adds unpredictability (reactive direction changes); in-game adds pressure + scanning + movement abilities. KovaaK's cannot replicate competitive pressure or active scanning — the foundation must be solid enough to absorb those in-game.
- **Do not let the player snap on direction changes.** Edge tracking: when the bot changes direction, ease back into the edge — don't snap. Snapping produces an abrupt correction that destabilizes. The reference for smooth edge tracking is Ricky/Hydrobody, who overcomes slightly then eases back. When the bot comes close, the movement doesn't change — it just looks faster because it's closer. Don't dramatize the approach; keep the same tension.
- **Do not let the player stutter corrections.** Commit to one smooth correction — don't begin it then stutter as you end it. A stutter at the end of a correction is a "speed bump" at the wrist pivot point: smooth tracking suddenly skips a large distance. One correction, committed start to finish.
- **Do not let flicks become floaty.** A floaty flick lacks urgency — instead of one decisive flick, the player flows toward the target. After flicking, always actively confirm whether on target — micro as part of the flick, not as a separate delayed step. Don't slow down, land up short, then micro; that's two movements, not one.
- **Excel reading: pay attention to acceleration/deceleration, not just direction.** When the bot excels away, it looks smaller on screen — a wide movement that hits at close range will cross over the bot at distance because the angular displacement is smaller. Engage more fingertips for the smaller angular correction; sticking with upper wrist and arm causes crossover. The player must read when the bot begins to slow down, not just whether it's going left or right.
- **KovaaK's cannot replicate competitive pressure or active scanning.** FFA Aim Arena has three loads KovaaK's doesn't: unpredictability, competitive pressure, and active scanning + movement-ability geometry changes. The progression accounts for this by building the foundation solid enough to absorb each layer in-game. Do not claim a KovaaK's routine "fixes" the FFA spike — it builds the baseline that makes the spike less likely. The VAXTA bridge and FFA practice are where pressure and scanning are trained, not KovaaK's.

## Tension management integration

When the player reports tension rising over a match (not just at one moment), apply MattyOW's tension-budget model:

- **Tension is a budget, not a binary.** Each muscle group (arm, wrist, fingertips) has an independent budget. Using one doesn't exhaust the others — but never releasing any of them leads to gradual lockout.
- **Release tension at direction changes, don't counteract it.** When the target decelerates, let the mouse glide to a near-stop by releasing tension, then redirect in the new direction. Do not fight old momentum with new tension.
- MattyOW describes muscle groups as having separate tension budgets, but treat this as an explanatory model, not an instruction to consciously route joints. Don't infer from this user's finger clenching that a particular arm/wrist budget is depleted.
- **Habitually release tension between engagements.** After a high-tensity exchange, consciously relax before the next one. Nerves encourage holding tension; the habit of releasing is what refills the budget.
- **Minimize tension changes during long strafes.** Any change in tension (how much or where) introduces jitter. Keep tension constant during sustained tracking.
- MattyOW distinguishes lateral from downward grip forces and notes that downward pressure can make tracking feel sticky. Treat this as a cue to experiment gently, not a universal grip prescription; don't ask the player to squeeze the mouse.
- **Finger clenching is an observable warning signal for this player.** Use it as a cue to pause or end the block; it does not prove the cause of their aim errors or which muscle group is responsible.
- **Edge tracking: ease back into the edge, don't snap.** When the bot changes direction, the correction should be an ease-back into the edge of the target, not a snap. Snapping destabilizes the tracking. The bot coming close does not mean it's moving faster — it just looks faster because it's closer. Keep the same tension regardless of bot distance.
- **Excel reading: the player must read acceleration/deceleration, not just direction.** Pay attention to when the bot begins to slow down — it's not uniform. When the bot excels away, it appears smaller on screen; a movement that would hit at close range will cross over the bot at distance because the angular displacement shrinks. Engage fingertips for the smaller angular correction at distance — sticking with upper wrist and arm causes crossover.
- **Commit to one smooth correction; do not stutter.** A correction that starts then stutters at the end is a wrist-pivot "speed bump" — smooth tracking suddenly skips a large distance. One correction, committed start to finish.
- **Wrist pivot exhaustion: the wrist has an anatomical range-of-motion ceiling.** When wrist pivot space runs out, the upper wrist lags behind — like a car fishtailing. This makes the next motion harder to initiate. Lay the hand cam and check whether upper-wrist-initiated movement is happening; it's a ROM ceiling, not a tension issue.
