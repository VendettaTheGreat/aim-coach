## 1. Fingertip vs. Upper Muscle Group — Space Exhaustion Cascade [NEW]

**Key diagnosis (53:05–54:45):** Watching the subscriber's hand cam during VT Controlsphere Novice, Matty identified that the player's micro corrections were NOT being initiated by the fingertips. At 34cm/360 sensitivity:

1. The player runs out of mouse pad space during micro corrections.
2. When out of space, the player engages an **upper muscle group** (wrist/forearm) to compensate — "to support me."
3. This upper-muscle engagement produces a **burst of speed** that gets the crosshair back on target quickly.
4. BUT: the correction is **not stabilized, not controlled, and not fingertip-engaged.** It's fast but sloppy.
5. The fix: **increase sensitivity** (drop from 34cm to ~30cm/360) to **force** more fingertip usage, because larger muscle groups alone can't make fine adjustments at high sens.

**This is distinct from the existing tension-budget model.** The tension model describes how to manage tension across muscle groups during sustained tracking. This is a **mechanical cascade**: space exhaustion → upper muscle engagement → burst speed without control → instability. The fix is sensitivity-driven, not tension-management-driven.

**Additional nuance (1:02:24):** When first trying to engage fingertips more, the player will look slightly more hesitant getting on the bot. This is expected — it's the effect of using a lower-range muscle group (fingertips) more than usual. Not a problem, just an adaptation phase.

## 2. Perception Overreaction — "Movement Feels Faster Than It Is" [NEW]

**Key diagnosis (49:02–50:37):** Matty identified a pattern where the player overreacts to target movement:

1. The player **perceives** target movement as faster than it actually is.
2. This perception causes **more tension in the upper hand/forearm/wrist** — and less attention to fingertips.
3. VT Controlsphere Novice is a scenario type that **can be easily controlled by fingertips**, but the player doesn't consider that because the movement *feels* faster than it is.
4. Visual clutter + multitasking (building, movement management) in-game compounds this — same overreaction transfers to game.

**Matty's breakdown of aim components:**
- ~50% is **mental**
- ~20% is **perception** (what you're seeing and how well you're seeing it)
- The rest is mechanical

**This is distinct from existing content.** The tension-management reference covers *managing* tension during sustained tracking. This covers the **root cause** of why players over-tense in the first place: a perceptual misjudgment of target speed that cascades into upper-muscle tension. The fix isn't just "release tension" — it's **recognizing that the movement is slower than you think** and that the task is fingertip-controllable.

**Flick-oriented players (48:17–48:56):** Common in Fortnite due to shotgun spam. When they fall off target, they react abruptly — snapping back instead of smoothly correcting. This doesn't promote good technique or stability. The abrupt reaction is both a mechanical and perceptual problem.

## 3. Movement-Compounded Overaim [NEW]

**Key diagnosis (1:11:11–1:12:48):** From the Fortnite gameplay VOD review:

1. Player is **wrist-oriented** (identified from training VODs).
2. When already **ulnar-deviated** (pushed to the right edge of the pad), engaging a movement onto a target uses the wrist — fine for speed, compromises control.
3. **Player movement compounds the aim error:** if you commit to holding W (forward) but take a lateral step during a micro correction, the distance your aim traverses = micro correction distance + lateral step distance. The step *adds to the aim distance.*
4. The fix isn't just aim-related — it's **positioning before the engagement.** Don't put yourself in a position where you have to ulnar-deviate to make the shot (1:09:49).

**This is distinct from existing content.** The tension model covers in-game angle snapping (lead with arm so wrist/fingers stay free). This covers how **player WASD movement during aim corrections amplifies the aim error** — a cross-contraction-like effect where keyboard input degrades mouse precision through physical distance addition, not just tension.

## 4. Routine Building Methodology [NEW]

**Matty's process (21:00, 1:36:00):**
1. Pick a subscriber randomly each month.
2. Review their VODs (both aim trainer AND gameplay).
3. Build the routine **blind** (no pre-review of scenarios) as a coaching exercise.
4. Make it public.

**Scenario selection logic:**
- Based on **what angles the game forces you to aim at** (23:48). Fortnite = high overheads + peripheral vision + wide-angle corrections (26:42).
- At high FOV, you make **wider angular corrections** than normal scenarios — so select scenarios with those geometry demands.
- **Good scenarios don't expire** (38:16) — if a scenario is really good, it doesn't matter if it's dated.
- **Fire rate on a scenario can encourage smoothness** (38:52) — the scenario mechanics themselves promote good technique.

**Difficulty diagnosis framework (46:07):**
Two possible reasons a scenario is difficult for a player:
1. **Not blending muscle groups** together to promote good smoothness (mechanical).
2. A perceptual/reading issue (explored later) — the movement feels faster than it is.

**Routine ordering principle (1:35:35–1:35:48):**
- **Reflex/speed first**, then dynamic/precision later.
- Work on speed first, introduce precision demand later.
- Control bleeds well into passive tracking — control-oriented scenarios first, then arc/bounce/dodge, then reflex.
- Structure: control → arc tracking → bounce sphere → dodge tracking → reflex/click.

**Don't play scenarios with "classic aim trainer control" (1:15:36):** On scored scenarios, don't over-control — almost MBM it (move-by-mouse) even if it's a scored scenario. Keep the wrist locked to train it well.

## 5. Sensitivity as a Coaching Tool [NEW]

**Key insight:** Matty uses sensitivity adjustment as an active coaching intervention, not just a personal preference:

- **Increase sensitivity** to force fingertip usage (54:40, 55:24). At higher sens, larger muscle groups can't make fine adjustments — fingertips are forced into the equation.
- **Decrease sensitivity** for scenarios that demand more pad space (Smoothbot, 57cm — "genuine infinite mouse pad space" at 17:07).
- The player's **neutral reset position on the pad** matters (1:05:15) — if you already reset near the edge, you have less room before deviation becomes a problem.

**This is distinct from existing content.** The existing tension reference mentions "train at slightly higher sens than comfortable to develop wrist/fingertip control" — but Matty here makes it an **explicit diagnostic tool**: watching hand cam, seeing micros aren't fingertip-initiated, prescribing a specific sens increase (-4cm/360) as the fix.

## 6. Leaking Shots / Overaim on Moving Targets [NEW]

**Key diagnosis (1:18:29–1:19:01):** During clicking scenarios, the player "leaks shots" — over-leads the target. When forced to take a micro correction from behind a moving target, the player tends to miss by overaiming and chasing down the target.

**Fix (1:19:01):** The selected clicking scenario helps because you **can't lead shots** — it forces micro correction without overaim. The scenario's mechanics prevent the error pattern.

**Don't chase targets (57:52):** If you miss and go after it, it disrupts rhythm. Specific to scenarios where target rhythm matters.

## 10. Application to This Player (Gabriel, 20cm/360, Wallhack SP-004)

**Sensitivity context:** At 20cm/360, the space-exhaustion cascade (§1) does NOT apply — the player is already at a sensitivity where fingertips are forced into micro corrections and pad space is not the constraint. However, the perception-overreaction finding (§2) applies with **amplified stakes**:

- At 20cm/360, 1mm of jitter = ~18° of crosshair displacement. At the subscriber's 34cm, the same 1mm = ~10.6°. Tension spikes produce nearly **double** the crosshair displacement per unit of tension compared to the diagnosed player.
- **The grip spike is amplified by sensitivity, not caused by it.** The root cause is perceptual: in-game visual complexity (abilities, effects, unpredictable movement) makes target speed feel faster than it is → upper-hand tension → grip spike → amplified displacement at 20cm.

**Diagnostic insight (user-reported, Oct 11):** "Targets in VAXTA (a controlled environment) feel much easier to aim at — in-game, players are actively using their abilities." This is the perception-overreaction pattern Matty described (§2): VAXTA = clean perception → accurate speed judgment → tension matches → no spike. In-game = cluttered perception → speed overestimate → tension overshoot → grip spike → amplified at 20cm → tracking degrades.

**Revised diagnosis chain:**
1. In-game visual complexity overloads perception (not a mechanical deficit).
2. Target speed is misperceived as faster than it is.
3. Upper-hand tension increases to match the *perceived* (not actual) speed.
4. Grip spike at 20cm/360 → ~1.8x crosshair displacement vs lower-sens players.
5. Tension doesn't fully release between encounters → budget drains over the match.
6. Tracking degrades as budget depletes — but the root cause was perceptual overload, not mechanical failure.

**Why Skill 1 foundation work is more important than it appeared:** It's not just training speed-matching mechanics — it's training **accurate speed perception in a clean environment** so the calibrated perception resists the in-game overload. The Skill 1 → Skill 2 → in-game progression should be understood as **progressively adding perceptual load**, not just reactivity or speed. The foundation builds the perceptual baseline that makes the in-game perception tax survivable.

**Revised cue for Skill 1 scenarios:** "The movement is slower than it feels — match it with tension, not speed. If you feel your grip tighten, the target didn't get faster, your perception did."
