## Smooth tracking foundations

- Smooth tracking is continuous matching of the target's current motion; it is not a series of panic flicks at every reversal.
- Watch the target and its movement cues, not only the crosshair. When the crosshair leaves target center, make a controlled micro-correction rather than continuing the old path off-target.
- Avoid pre-empting a direction change before it is visible. First learn to follow the current movement and react smoothly; prediction can be useful in a game, but it is not a substitute for learning the tracking response.
- Develop smooth tracking as a foundation that supports reactive tracking and game aiming. Maintain crosshair uptime without forcing unnecessary corrections.
- Reacquire and correct smoothly when the bot changes direction; avoid hard flicks that land where the bot used to be.

## Reactive-tracking progression

- Reactive tracking builds on smooth-tracking fundamentals: speed matching, target reading, efficient correction, and smooth response.
- Distinguish controlled smooth reversals (e.g. Controlsphere in the supplied Voltaic sheet) from reactive acceleration. For Overwatch-like sharp ground accelerations, consider an easy Ground Plaza Sparky variant; the official `Ground Plaza Sparky v3 OW Easier` listing describes instant ground acceleration, thinner bots, and no player movement. Add it after the smooth foundation, starting at the easier version; it is a reactive tracking layer, not a WASD dodge task.
- Use bot profiles with frequent, less predictable acceleration changes when the aim of a block is reading/reactivity. Highly predictable strafe profiles can be memorized or gamed, so high score alone may not show improved reading.
- If reactive tasks are currently too demanding, reduce difficulty or choose a more manageable variant; don't force fast/hard scenarios just because they are reactive.
- However, larger targets can make it easier to rely on prediction and inflate performance in a reading-focused task. Keep target size/task difficulty appropriate to the specific goal: larger/easier can isolate basic mechanics; later reading practice should not be trivialized by an overly generous target.
- Treat any numerical accuracy threshold mentioned in a video as an example/heuristic, not a universal progression gate. Use scenario-specific difficulty and observed control.

## Keep the training dimensions separate

When selecting a scenario, identify independently:
1. target movement geometry (ground, vertical, wide arc, frequent reversals);
2. player movement (stationary aim, voluntary WASD/dodge, or engine-forced movement);
3. aim task (smooth tracking, reactive tracking, target switching, clicking);
4. target size / precision demand;
5. speed and named scenario difficulty.

Change one dimension at a time. A scenario can be easy in speed but hard in precision due to a small/thin target. A movement-dodge task can still be predominantly tracking, but it adds a player-movement demand. These distinctions should guide where it sits in a progression.

## Application to reports of tension

- Use low-demand smooth scenarios first to establish whether tension rises during sustained tracking or specifically at direction changes.
- Add reactive variability only after the player can follow the easier movement without the grip clenching and aim deterioration returning quickly.
- Do not tell the player to consciously route shoulder, wrist, and fingers simultaneously. Use an external cue (watch and follow the target smoothly) and record what happens.
- Finger clenching is an observed warning signal in this user's report; it does not, by itself, prove the cause of tracking errors or prove which muscle group is fatigued.
