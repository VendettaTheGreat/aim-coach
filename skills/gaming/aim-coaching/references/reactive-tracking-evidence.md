# Reactive Tracking: Principle-to-Evidence Guide

Use this as the neutral framework for reactive-tracking coaching. The aim-community concepts below are hypotheses and useful task descriptions, not established physiological categories. Do not center player identities, named playstyles, or coach-specific labels in a diagnosis. Extract the observable demand and test the competing movement strategies against that demand.

## What reactive tracking requires

In a continuous tracking task, the player observes target motion, moves the crosshair, and updates that movement as target velocity and direction change. Diagnose the failure point rather than calling every miss a “reaction-time problem”:

- **Lag:** crosshair falls behind a moving target or takes too long to regain it.
- **Overshoot / reversal error:** crosshair continues in the previous direction after the target changes direction.
- **Reacquisition loss:** crosshair leaves the target and returns through a second, third, or large correction.
- **Poor speed match:** crosshair stays near the target but its relative position oscillates or drifts.
- **Axis-specific loss:** control is adequate horizontally but breaks down vertically, or vice versa.
- **Input coupling:** mouse control degrades during player movement or simultaneous keyboard input.
- **Load-related loss:** these errors emerge or worsen with time, tension, or fatigue.

“Visual reading,” response latency, mouse movement, target geometry, and tension are separate candidate contributors. A scenario score alone does not isolate them.

## Reconcile the competing reactive-tracking proposals

Two supplied aim-training sources describe different choices at target changes: smoothly integrate a correction into tracking versus quickly reacquire the target and resume matching its motion. They also disagree about how much to offset the crosshair within a target. Treat those as conditional hypotheses, not opposing universal rules.

### 1. Smooth correction versus separate reacquisition

- When the crosshair is still on or near the target, begin with one simple external cue: **“Keep the crosshair with the target as it changes direction.”** A continuous pursuit response avoids adding a large, unnecessary displacement to a small error.
- When the crosshair has clearly lost the target, a quicker separate reposition may reduce the time off-target. It can also overshoot, prolong reacquisition, or interrupt the motion information being followed. Do not call this a flick prescription; test whether it actually reduces error and recovery time for the player's task.
- The scientific literature supports continuous feedback control with unavoidable sensorimotor delay, and shows that motion extrapolation can compensate for delay in manual tracking. It does **not** establish that smooth correction or a separate fast correction is universally superior for FPS aim. These are task- and error-size-dependent options, not a personality or “playstyle” category.

### 2. Crosshair position within the target

- Center tracking and edge-biased tracking are alternatives for managing different costs: immediate crosshair-on-target margin versus earlier access to a directional/axis cue or additional room for an expected displacement.
- A video proposal to track nearer the horizontal center while biasing vertically is a useful testable geometry hypothesis, not a general scientific rule. A vertical offset may sacrifice hit margin; its value depends on target size, movement direction, acceleration, and the game’s hitbox/weapon demands.
- Default to a neutral target-focused cue. Only test an offset when observations identify a specific axis or reversal problem, and compare it with centered tracking under the same target path and difficulty.

### 3. Reading acceleration and reversal

- Do not infer that an error is slow reaction time just because it occurs near a reversal. Separate the target’s visible deceleration/acceleration, the crosshair’s own momentum, direction of the error, and how long reacquisition takes.
- Practice on the target-motion geometry that matters. Predictable motion can support anticipation; unpredictable motion makes a memorized schedule unreliable. Prediction can compensate for sensory-motor delays, but over-predicting an unseen reversal can create premature movement. Cue reacting to visible motion unless the game/task supplies a reliable advance cue.
- Generic reaction-time scores are not a substitute for end-to-end performance on a representative tracking task. A deficit may lie in reading, response initiation, movement calibration, braking, or their interaction.

### 4. Tension and movement strategy

- Do not teach increased grip or muscle tension as a universal way to react faster. Tension can be recorded as a player-reported/observed state; keep the existing light-grip ceiling and stop/reset rules. Do not route body parts or prescribe coach/person-specific styles.
- Keep WASD/player locomotion distinct from target movement. First identify whether the scenario requires mouse-only tracking, player dodge, or both; test keyboard/mouse coupling only when the game task includes it.
- Keep click-timing separate: use smooth pre-tracking and a quiet click; never apply a reactive-tracking idea as a flick/snap instruction for clicking.

## Practical test and measures

Change one task demand at a time (target path, speed, size, verticality, player movement, or correction cue). Compare the same condition over repeat sessions. Track at least one observable outcome, not just score:

1. time or frequency with crosshair off-target;
2. lag versus overshoot at direction changes;
3. number/size of corrections needed to reacquire;
4. whether the error is horizontal, vertical, or both;
5. grip spike or keyboard-coupled disruption, if reported/observed.

A faster correction is only an improvement if it reduces the task-relevant error without causing more overshoot, time off target, tension, or loss of control. For transfer, include a short representative game task when workload allows; aim-trainer improvement alone is not evidence of game transfer.

## Evidence calibration

- **Visual pursuit / continuous control:** smooth pursuit is a visually guided control process with both sensory and predictive contributions. Manual tracking research also models sensorimotor delays and compensation through motion extrapolation. This supports treating tracking as ongoing feedback control rather than a series of independent reaction-time tests. These studies are not FPS mouse-aim trials.
- **Continuous and intermittent corrections can coexist:** eye-hand tracking research reports intermittent components in target-directed manual tracking. Therefore, “smooth” versus “corrective” is not necessarily a binary choice; the size and timing of corrections should be evaluated from observable error.
- **Practice variability and transfer:** a 2024 contextual-interference meta-analysis reports an overall transfer advantage for random practice, but the applied-setting estimate was small and statistically non-significant, with substantial heterogeneity. Three scenario variants are a practical option, not a guaranteed learning benefit. Keep variability within the same required skill/geometry and use it only when control remains stable.
- **Task-specific perceptual training:** a 2024 team-sport review describes transfer as sensitive to how closely training stimuli and required responses match the performance task. This supports validating transfer in the actual game context; it does not establish that aim-trainer drills automatically transfer to FPS play.
- **Attentional focus:** a 2021 meta-analysis found external-focus instructions favored motor performance, retention, and transfer over internal/body-part focus across included motor tasks. This supports concise target/effect cues rather than joint micromanagement, while not proving an identical effect size for FPS aim.
